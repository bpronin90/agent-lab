THE DOOM METER (highly subjective, not a forecast)

Real disruption at the entry level, measured. Mass replacement of whole

WHAT THE DOOMERS GET RIGHT
--------------------------
* [MEASURED] Early-career workers in the most AI-exposed occupations
have already lost ground in employment relative to others. [1]
    "early-career workers (ages 22-25) in the most AI-exposed
    occupations have experienced a 13 percent relative decline in
    employment"
    Fine print: This is a relative decline in payroll data, and it is
    early evidence. Experienced workers in the same jobs held steady or
    grew.
    Translation: The robots did not storm the partners' offices. They
    quietly took the intern's stapler.
* [FORECAST] Most U.S. workers have some tasks exposed to large language
models, and about one in five could see at least half of their tasks
affected. [2]
    "around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their
    work tasks affected by the introduction of LLMs, while approximately
    19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted"
    Fine print: This measures task exposure, not job loss. The authors
    say it does not predict whether jobs are eliminated or how fast
    adoption happens.
    Translation: Exposure is like saying a lot of your kitchen could be
    affected by a microwave. True! You still did not fire the chef.
* [FORECAST] A major bank forecast that a large share of legal tasks
could be automated. [3]
    "We estimate that 44% of tasks in legal professions could be
    automated."
    Fine print: This is a model-based estimate from an investment bank,
    not a measurement. The same report says most exposed jobs are more
    likely to be complemented than substituted.
* [MEASURED] In a field experiment, consultants using AI on a task
outside the AI's abilities were less likely to get it right than
consultants without AI. [4]
    "For a task selected to be outside the frontier, consultants using
    AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct
    solutions than those without AI."
    Fine print: These were consultants, not lawyers or accountants. But
    the pattern of unevenness is the professional-judgment problem in
    miniature.
    Translation: The AI is a power drill. Great for holes. Terrible for
    brain surgery. Knowing which job you are doing is the job.

REASONS TO CHILL
----------------
* [MEASURED] Despite widespread adoption, AI chatbots showed no
significant effect on earnings or recorded hours in any occupation
studied. [5]
    "AI chatbots have had no significant impact on earnings or recorded
    hours in any occupation"
    Fine print: This covers one country (Denmark) and an early adoption
    period. Effects could grow as tools improve.
    Translation: Everyone bought the fancy air fryer. Dinner,
    statistically speaking, is the same dinner.
* [MEASURED] A federal court sanctioned lawyers who filed a brief citing
judicial opinions that did not exist, which had been generated by
ChatGPT. [6]
    "the court sanctioned two lawyers and their firm, imposing a $5,000
    penalty, after they submitted a brief citing judicial opinions that
    did not exist and had been generated by ChatGPT"
    Fine print: One case. It shows accountability lands on humans. It
    does not show that AI tools are useless in legal work.
    Translation: You can let the GPS pick the route. If you drive into
    the lake, the judge still talks to you, not the GPS.
* [MEASURED] General-purpose models hallucinated badly when asked
verifiable questions about federal court cases. [7]
    "legal hallucinations are alarmingly prevalent, occurring between
    69% of the time with ChatGPT 3.5 and 88% with Llama 2"
    Fine print: These are older, general models, not specialized legal
    tools, and newer systems may do better. The point is that
    verification is still a human job.
    Translation: Imagine a paralegal who is brilliant, tireless, and
    confidently invents a Supreme Court case before lunch.
* [MEASURED] The American Bar Association says lawyers using generative
AI must consider their ethical duties, including competence and
protecting client information. [8]
    "Lawyers using generative artificial intelligence tools must fully
    consider their applicable ethical obligations, including their
    duties to provide competent legal representation, to protect client
    information"
    Fine print: Ethics rules shape how AI is used. They do not by
    themselves protect headcount, and this release is guidance, not a
    statute.
    Translation: The robot can draft the contract. The robot cannot be
    disbarred, which, from a client's point of view, is a bug.
* [MEASURED] After ATMs were widely deployed, the number of full-time-
equivalent bank tellers grew. [9]
    "Since ATMs were widely deployed, the number of full-time-equivalent
    bank tellers has grown."
    Fine print: Teller employment later declined as mobile banking
    spread. Disruption delayed is still disruption.
    Translation: The cash robot arrived, and the humans got promoted to
    'person you talk to about your mortgage.' Then the phone app showed
    up. Plot twist.
* [FORECAST] U.S. government projections still expect accountant and
auditor employment to grow over the decade. [10]
    "Employment of accountants and auditors is projected to grow 6
    percent from 2023 to 2033, faster than the average for all
    occupations."
    Fine print: BLS projections are not designed to anticipate rapid
    technology shocks and can be wrong.

ALSO IN THE FILE
----------------
* [MEASURED] In a large workplace study, a generative AI assistant
raised productivity, mostly for less-experienced workers. [11]
* [SPECULATION] An economist's review of workplace automation finds it
also complements labor and can raise demand for it. [12]

WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO
-------------------
-> Become the person who checks the machine. Verifying AI output is now
a billable skill, and courts are actively punishing people who skip it.
[6][7]
-> Learn where the tools are great and where they faceplant. That jagged
edge is where professional judgment earns its money. [4]
-> If you are early in your career, aim at the parts of the job that
involve clients, judgment and accountability, since entry-level routine
work is where the pressure shows up first. [1][11]
-> Treat scary percentages as task exposure, not pink slips, and ask
every headline: measured, or forecast? [2][3]

THE VERDICT
-----------
The robots are coming for tasks, especially the junior, repetitive ones,
and that pressure is real and already measurable. They are not, on
current evidence, replacing whole professions that run on judgment,
liability and trust. Nobody can promise that holds forever. But 'the job
changes' is a very different movie from 'the job ends', and right now
the evidence is watching the first one. Calm down. Then go learn the
tools.

STILL UNCERTAIN
- Most labor-market evidence covers the first couple of years of
adoption. Effects could grow as tools improve.
- Entry-level declines could shrink the pipeline of future senior
professionals, an effect that would take years to show.
- Task-exposure forecasts disagree with each other and with measured
outcomes so far.
