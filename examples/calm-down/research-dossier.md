## Demonstrated facts

### F2: Early-career workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have already lost ground in employment relative to others.

- **stance:** supports_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S4
- **evidence:** "early-career workers (ages 22-25) in the most AI-exposed occupations have experienced a 13 percent relative decline in employment"
- **caveat:** This is a relative decline in payroll data, and it is early evidence. Experienced workers in the same jobs held steady or grew.

### F4: Despite widespread adoption, AI chatbots showed no significant effect on earnings or recorded hours in any occupation studied.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S3
- **evidence:** "AI chatbots have had no significant impact on earnings or recorded hours in any occupation"
- **caveat:** This covers one country (Denmark) and an early adoption period. Effects could grow as tools improve.

### F5: A federal court sanctioned lawyers who filed a brief citing judicial opinions that did not exist, which had been generated by ChatGPT.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** high
- **sources:** S5
- **evidence:** "the court sanctioned two lawyers and their firm, imposing a $5,000 penalty, after they submitted a brief citing judicial opinions that did not exist and had been generated by ChatGPT"
- **caveat:** One case. It shows accountability lands on humans. It does not show that AI tools are useless in legal work.

### F6: General-purpose models hallucinated badly when asked verifiable questions about federal court cases.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S6
- **evidence:** "legal hallucinations are alarmingly prevalent, occurring between 69% of the time with ChatGPT 3.5 and 88% with Llama 2"
- **caveat:** These are older, general models, not specialized legal tools, and newer systems may do better. The point is that verification is still a human job.

### F7: In a field experiment, consultants using AI on a task outside the AI's abilities were less likely to get it right than consultants without AI.

- **stance:** mixed · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S7
- **evidence:** "For a task selected to be outside the frontier, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions than those without AI."
- **caveat:** These were consultants, not lawyers or accountants. But the pattern of unevenness is the professional-judgment problem in miniature.

### F8: In a large workplace study, a generative AI assistant raised productivity, mostly for less-experienced workers.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S2
- **evidence:** "Access to the tool increases productivity, as measured by issues resolved per hour, by 14 percent on average, with the greatest impact on novice and low-skilled workers"
- **caveat:** This was customer support, not law or accounting. Productivity gains can still reduce hiring.

### F10: After ATMs were widely deployed, the number of full-time-equivalent bank tellers grew.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S9
- **evidence:** "Since ATMs were widely deployed, the number of full-time-equivalent bank tellers has grown."
- **caveat:** Teller employment later declined as mobile banking spread. Disruption delayed is still disruption.

### F11: The American Bar Association says lawyers using generative AI must consider their ethical duties, including competence and protecting client information.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** high
- **sources:** S10
- **evidence:** "Lawyers using generative artificial intelligence tools must fully consider their applicable ethical obligations, including their duties to provide competent legal representation, to protect client information"
- **caveat:** Ethics rules shape how AI is used. They do not by themselves protect headcount, and this release is guidance, not a statute.

## Forecasts and exposure estimates

### F1: Most U.S. workers have some tasks exposed to large language models, and about one in five could see at least half of their tasks affected.

- **stance:** supports_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S1
- **evidence:** "around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by the introduction of LLMs, while approximately 19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted"
- **caveat:** This measures task exposure, not job loss. The authors say it does not predict whether jobs are eliminated or how fast adoption happens.

### F3: A major bank forecast that a large share of legal tasks could be automated.

- **stance:** supports_fear · **confidence:** low
- **sources:** S11
- **evidence:** "We estimate that 44% of tasks in legal professions could be automated."
- **caveat:** This is a model-based estimate from an investment bank, not a measurement. The same report says most exposed jobs are more likely to be complemented than substituted.

### F12: U.S. government projections still expect accountant and auditor employment to grow over the decade.

- **stance:** challenges_fear · **confidence:** medium
- **sources:** S12
- **evidence:** "Employment of accountants and auditors is projected to grow 6 percent from 2023 to 2033, faster than the average for all occupations."
- **caveat:** BLS projections are not designed to anticipate rapid technology shocks and can be wrong.

## Uncertainty

- Most labor-market evidence covers the first couple of years of adoption. Effects could grow as tools improve.
- Entry-level declines could shrink the pipeline of future senior professionals, an effect that would take years to show.
- Task-exposure forecasts disagree with each other and with measured outcomes so far.
