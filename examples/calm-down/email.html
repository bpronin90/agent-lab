😬 What the Doomers Get Right
MEASURED  point for the robots · confidence: medium ★ SURPRISING
Early-career workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have already lost ground in employment relative to others.[2]
“early-career workers (ages 22-25) in the most AI-exposed occupations have experienced a 13 percent relative decline in employment”
Fine print: This is a relative decline in payroll data, and it is early evidence. Experienced workers in the same jobs held steady or grew.
🍝 Translation: The robots did not storm the partners' offices. They quietly took the intern's stapler.
FORECAST  point for the robots · confidence: medium ★ SURPRISING
Most U.S. workers have some tasks exposed to large language models, and about one in five could see at least half of their tasks affected.[1]
“around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by the introduction of LLMs, while approximately 19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted”
Fine print: This measures task exposure, not job loss. The authors say it does not predict whether jobs are eliminated or how fast adoption happens.
🍝 Translation: Exposure is like saying a lot of your kitchen could be affected by a microwave. True! You still did not fire the chef.
FORECAST  point for the robots · confidence: low
A major bank forecast that a large share of legal tasks could be automated.[7]
“We estimate that 44% of tasks in legal professions could be automated.”
Fine print: This is a model-based estimate from an investment bank, not a measurement. The same report says most exposed jobs are more likely to be complemented than substituted.
MEASURED  it's complicated · confidence: medium ★ SURPRISING
In a field experiment, AI made consultants faster and better on some tasks and worse on a task outside its abilities.[5]
“For a task selected to be outside the frontier, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions than those without AI.”
Fine print: These were consultants, not lawyers or accountants. But the pattern of unevenness is the professional-judgment problem in miniature.
🍝 Translation: The AI is a power drill. Great for holes. Terrible for brain surgery. Knowing which job you are doing is the job.

🧊 Reasons to Chill
MEASURED  point for the humans · confidence: medium ★ SURPRISING
In Danish administrative data that covers exposed occupations including accountants, AI chatbots showed no significant effect on earnings or hours.[3]
“AI chatbots have had no significant impact on earnings or recorded hours in any occupation”
Fine print: This covers one country and an early adoption period. Effects could grow as tools improve.
🍝 Translation: Everyone bought the fancy air fryer. Dinner, statistically speaking, is the same dinner.
MEASURED  point for the humans · confidence: high
Lawyers who filed AI-invented case citations were sanctioned by a federal court, which said the attorneys stay responsible for their filings.[8]
“the court sanctioned two lawyers and their firm, imposing a $5,000 penalty, after they submitted a brief citing judicial opinions that did not exist and had been generated by ChatGPT”
Fine print: One case. It shows accountability lands on humans. It does not show that AI tools are useless in legal work.
🍝 Translation: You can let the GPS pick the route. If you drive into the lake, the judge still talks to you, not the GPS.
MEASURED  point for the humans · confidence: medium ★ SURPRISING
General-purpose models hallucinated badly when asked verifiable questions about federal court cases.[4]
“legal hallucinations are alarmingly prevalent, occurring between 69% of the time with ChatGPT 3.5 and 88% with Llama 2”
Fine print: These are older, general models, not specialized legal tools, and newer systems may do better. The point is that verification is still a human job.
🍝 Translation: Imagine a paralegal who is brilliant, tireless, and confidently invents a Supreme Court case before lunch.
MEASURED  point for the humans · confidence: high
The American Bar Association says lawyers using generative AI keep their duties of competence, confidentiality, supervision and reasonable fees.[9]
“Lawyers using generative artificial intelligence tools must fully consider their applicable ethical obligations, including their duties to provide competent legal representation, to protect client information”
Fine print: Ethics rules shape how AI is used. They do not by themselves protect headcount.
🍝 Translation: The robot can draft the contract. The robot cannot be disbarred, which, from a client's point of view, is a bug.
MEASURED  point for the humans · confidence: medium ★ SURPRISING
After ATMs spread, the number of bank tellers grew because cheaper branches meant more branches.[6]
“Since ATMs were widely deployed, the number of full-time-equivalent bank tellers has grown.”
Fine print: Teller employment later declined as mobile banking spread. Disruption delayed is still disruption.
🍝 Translation: The cash robot arrived, and the humans got promoted to 'person you talk to about your mortgage.' Then the phone app showed up. Plot twist.
FORECAST  point for the humans · confidence: medium
U.S. government projections still expect accountant and auditor employment to grow over the decade.[10]
“Employment of accountants and auditors is projected to grow 6 percent from 2023 to 2033, faster than the average for all occupations.”
Fine print: BLS projections are not designed to anticipate rapid technology shocks and can be wrong.

🛠 What To Actually Do
→Become the person who checks the machine. Verifying AI output is now a billable skill, and courts are actively punishing people who skip it. based on F5, F6
→Learn where the tools are great and where they faceplant. That jagged edge is where professional judgment earns its money. based on F7
→If you are early in your career, aim at the parts of the job that involve clients, judgment and accountability, since entry-level routine work is where the pressure shows up first. based on F2, F8
→Treat scary percentages as task exposure, not pink slips, and ask every headline: measured, or forecast? based on F1, F3
THE VERDICT
The robots are coming for tasks, especially the junior, repetitive ones, and that pressure is real and already measurable. They are not, on current evidence, replacing whole professions that run on judgment, liability and trust. Nobody can promise that holds forever. But 'the job changes' is a very different movie from 'the job ends', and right now the evidence is watching the first one. Calm down. Then go learn the tools.

STILL UNCERTAIN
  • Most labor-market evidence covers the first couple of years of adoption. Effects could grow as tools improve.
  • Entry-level declines could shrink the pipeline of future senior professionals, an effect that would take years to show.
  • Task-exposure forecasts disagree with each other and with measured outcomes so far.
